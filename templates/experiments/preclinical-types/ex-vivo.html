Ex Vivo Experiments

Definition

Ex Vivo experiments are experiments performed on isolated organs or tissues removed from a living animal but maintained functional in an artificial physiological environment such as an organ bath.

Purpose: To study the effect of drugs on a specific tissue or organ while maintaining its natural physiological structure.

Step-by-Step Procedure

  • Animal is humanely euthanized (in actual lab; virtual in PCI syllabus).
  • Carefully dissect and remove the required organ or tissue (e.g., guinea-pig ileum, rat aorta, frog heart).
  • Mount the tissue in an organ bath filled with physiological salt solution such as Tyrode’s, Krebs, or Ringer’s solution.
  • Maintain proper conditions: 37°C temperature, aeration with 95% O₂ + 5% CO₂, and constant tension.
  • Connect the preparation to a transducer, kymograph, or digital recorder.
  • Allow the tissue to stabilize for 30–60 minutes.
  • Add the test drug in cumulative doses and record contraction or relaxation responses.
  • Wash the tissue and repeat with a standard drug.
  • Plot the dose-response curve and calculate pD₂ or EC₅₀.

Advantages & Limitations

Advantages

  • More physiological than in vitro experiments
  • Tissue architecture and natural receptors are preserved
  • Fewer animals required compared with in vivo studies
  • Very useful for studying drug mechanisms of action

Disadvantages

  • Tissue viability is limited (usually only a few hours)
  • Still requires sacrifice of experimental animals
